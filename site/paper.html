Silicon cavities for colour centres and fast electrons: a small-scale rebuild with quasinormal modes, perturbation theory and a master equation

Alejandro Guirado
Applicant, PhD scholarship in Silicon Quantum Electrodynamics at the Deep Nanoscale, DTU Electro (QUEST doctoral network)

Abstract

This document accompanies my application to the PhD scholarship in Silicon Quantum Electrodynamics at the Deep Nanoscale. The project designs silicon cavities with deep-subwavelength confinement, describes them with modal expansions, and connects them to fast electrons and to embedded colour centres. I build each step at small scale and test it against an exact or published result. Quasinormal-mode (QNM) expansions of a dielectric barrier reproduce the convergence published in [1] (error with QNMs, against from its fit), and a 2D finite-element QNM solver with perfectly matched layers agrees with the exact QNMs of a cylinder to . First-order QNM perturbation theory predicts the spread of the resonance under random fabrication errors. A passing electron couples best to the nanobeam mode at keV, and a single QNM reproduces its energy loss. A master-equation model of the G centre shows that the measured lifetimes of [2] require a quantum efficiency of at least , and that strong coupling needs the bowtie and Q above . All numbers are computed by code in the accompanying repository.

Purpose and scope

The call asks for the theory, design and modelling of silicon cavities with deep-subwavelength confinement, using numerical simulations and modal expansions of the electromagnetic field; designs with the specifications that fabrication needs, and models to interpret the measurements of the project partners; the interaction of cavity fields with fast electrons for transmission-electron-microscope experiments; and the quantum optics of such cavities with embedded colour centres. Sections 2–5 build each step at small scale, in two dimensions where the PhD would work in three. Table 1 maps the tasks to the sections; Table 2 lists the checks.

Built for this study: a 2D finite-element Maxwell solver (Nédélec elements of any order, NGSolve [12]) with perfectly matched layers, a shift-and-invert search for the QNMs and their PML normalisation [8]; QNM expansions of a transmission, a Purcell factor and an electron energy loss, each against a direct solution; first-order QNM perturbation theory for moving boundaries; a Lindblad master equation for a two-level emitter and one mode. unit tests run in continuous integration.

Quasinormal modes against exact results

Task: modal expansions of electromagnetic fields. A QNM solves Maxwell's equations without sources and radiates outwards; its frequency $\tilde\omega = \omega - i\gamma$ is complex and $Q = \omega/2\gamma$. Normalised QNMs expand the response of a resonator [1]. I check each piece against an exact result: the dielectric barrier of [1] in 1D (refractive index $n = \pi$, analytic QNMs); a 2D finite-element solver against the exact QNMs of a silicon cylinder ($n$ = 3.48, $R$ = 0.5 µm, in-plane electric field), whose QNMs are the roots of $J_m'(nkR)/[nJ_m(nkR)] = H_m'(kR)/H_m(kR)$; and the Purcell factor from the QNMs against the direct solution with a source.

Method. In 1D the QNMs are analytic [1]: $\tilde\omega_mL/c = [2\pi m + i\ln((n-1)^2/(n+1)^2)]/2n$, with field $e^{in\tilde k_mx} + (-1)^m e^{-in\tilde k_mx}$ inside the barrier and norm $(-1)^m2n^2L$. The total field is expanded as $E \approx \sum_m a_m\tilde f_m/(\omega - \tilde\omega_m)$, with $a_m$ from the incoming field at the two surfaces, and the scattered field with $b_m = -\tfrac{\omega}{2}\int\tilde f_m\,\Delta\varepsilon\,E_{\rm in}\,dx$ in place of $a_m$. In 2D the curl–curl equation is discretised with Nédélec elements, a radial PML closes the domain, and shift-and-invert Arnoldi on $(\mathsf A - \sigma\mathsf M)^{-1}\mathsf M$ finds the QNMs, normalised with $\mathbf e^T\mathsf M\mathbf e = 1$ (unconjugated, PML included [8]). Spurious PML modes are rejected by their weight in the PML.

Result. With three QNMs the transmission error has its minimum, , at $\omega L/c = 4$ (one QNM: ); [1] reports about 2% and 7%. The fitted convergence laws, (total field) and (scattered field), match the fits printed in [1]. The finite-element QNMs converge exponentially with the element order to (order ) for $Q$ from 10 to 14 000. At the Purcell peak, the QNM pair agrees with the direct solution to (radial dipole) and (azimuthal); away from the peak the azimuthal dipole also couples to modes the pair leaves out.

A silicon cavity for the G centre

Tasks: design with the specifications fabrication needs; models to interpret measurements. The starting point is the nanobeam with which [2] enhanced a single G centre: 220 nm silicon-on-insulator, holes of radius 115 nm and period 350 nm, a four-hole taper to 85 nm and 250 nm, beam width 520 nm (symmetric here, six mirror holes on each side). The membrane is modelled in 2D with the effective index $n_{\rm eff}$ = of its TE0 slab mode ($n_{\rm Si}$ = at 1278 nm [11]); all in-plane dimensions are scaled to put the cavity mode at 1278 nm. A silicon bowtie at the centre, left between two air trapezoids that replace the innermost holes, concentrates the field in a bridge of width $w$.

The two designs. $A = 1/{\rm Re}(\varepsilon\tilde f_y^2)$ at the centre for the normalised QNM; $V \approx A\,h_{\rm eff}$ with $h_{\rm eff}$ = nm.

Method. First-order perturbation theory for moving boundaries [7], written with unconjugated products of the normalised QNM, $\delta\tilde k = -\tfrac{\tilde k}{2}\oint h\,[\Delta\varepsilon\,\tilde f_\parallel^2 - \Delta(\varepsilon^{-1})\tilde D_\perp^2]\,dl$, gives the complex shift for any change of a hole or of the bridge. Summed over random hole errors, it predicts the distribution of the resonance, the approach of [6] for random surface deformations.

Result. The nanobeam mode volume is (λ/n)³, against 0.26 from the 3D FDTD calculation of [2]. The bowtie reduces the mode area times at $w$ = 10 nm; the area falls roughly in proportion to $w$, while the in-plane Q falls from about 105 to about 3 300. Halving the elements at the bridge or raising the order from 3 to 4 changes A by at most and Q by . Perturbation theory agrees with re-simulation to (all holes grown) and (bridge widened). As specifications: growing all holes by 1 nm shifts the nanobeam mode by nm, and widening the bridge by 1 nm shifts the bowtie mode by + nm. For σ = nm errors the spread of λ is nm (nanobeam) and nm (bowtie), against and nm predicted. In 3D, the bowtie of [3] reaches 0.083 (λ/2nSi)³; the 2D model cannot be compared with it directly, and its Q lacks the radiation out of the membrane (measured Q of the nanobeam of [2]: 4 600).

Fast electrons passing the cavity

Task: interaction of cavity fields with fast electrons. An electron moving at speed $v$ on a straight line exchanges energy with a mode through the Fourier component of the field at $q = \omega/v$ along its path [9]. The trajectory is aloof, in the plane of the membrane, parallel to the beam at a distance $b$ from its edge; in 2D the electron is a line charge. I compute the coupling integral $C(v) = \int\tilde f_x(x, y_e)e^{-i\omega x/v}dx$ of the normalised QNM, which sets the electron–photon coupling in photon-induced near-field electron microscopy [10], and the energy-loss spectrum $\Gamma(\omega) \propto -{\rm Re}\int dx\,e^{-iqx}E^{\rm ind}_x(x, y_e)$, directly (scattered-field FEM driven by the field of the moving charge, $\phi \propto e^{iqx - \kappa|y - y_e|}$ with $\kappa = q/\gamma$) and from the single QNM.

Result. The coupling peaks at keV (nanobeam) and keV (bowtie) for all three distances. The spatial spectrum peaks at $q$ = π/a, next to the band edge of the mirror, and phase matching $v = \omega a/\pi$ gives keV. Above the peak the bowtie keeps much more coupling (at 300 keV, of its maximum against ): the field concentrated at the bowtie has a broader spatial spectrum. Near the resonance the single QNM reproduces the loss peak to (nanobeam) and (bowtie); the direct spectrum is positive everywhere, as it must be for a passive structure. A line charge on an aloof path is the simplest geometry; trajectories near and through the membrane need the 3D field.

A G centre in the cavity

Task: quantum optics with embedded colour centres. The G centre emits at 1278 nm with a lifetime of 5.97 ns in the nanobeam outside the cavity, a Debye–Waller factor of 15% and a quantum efficiency ε of 1–10% [2]. From $\Gamma_{\rm ZPL} = {\rm DW}\,\varepsilon\,\Gamma_0 = n\omega^3\mu^2/(3\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar c^3)$, μ = D (ε = 1%) to D (10%); a mode volume $V$ gives $\hbar g = \mu\sqrt{\hbar\omega/2\varepsilon_0n^2V}$. Emitter and mode follow the Lindblad master equation of the Jaynes–Cummings model with cavity loss κ = ω/Q, emitter decay γ = 1/τ0 and pure dephasing γ*, set to GHz × 2π from the 0.091 nm linewidth measured in [2] (an upper bound).

Coupling strength for ε = 10%, at the measured Q = 4 600 of [2]. Strong coupling: $4g > \kappa + \gamma + 2\gamma^*$.

Outlook

3D.
The same QNM workflow for the full membrane, with the radiation out of the plane, a real Q, and mode volumes without the effective-index step.
Design.
The perturbation sensitivities of Section 3 are the gradients that inverse design needs; topology-optimised cavities as in [4].
Electrons.
3D trajectories near and through the membrane, and coupling constants for the electron-microscopy experiments of the network.
Emitters.
Phonon sideband and spectral diffusion of the G centre, several QNMs, and the coupled QNM frequencies of [5] from the 3D field.
